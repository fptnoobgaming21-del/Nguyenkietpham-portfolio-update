Chói (Shine)

The first album by Ánh Sáng AZA, redesigned as a complete vinyl packaging system in the visual language of Ukiyo-e.

01 / The brief

A modern album through a historical lens.

What was the problem?

The assignment asked us to recreate a modern media project in the visual language of a historical design period discussed in class. I chose Chói (Shine), the first album by Ánh Sáng AZA, and Ukiyo-e from Japan's Edo period.

Ukiyo-e tells stories visually: flat areas of color, bold outlines, decorative borders, controlled composition and an expressive use of nature. The challenge was to translate that language into a contemporary album without copying one specific artist, and without losing the soft, youthful identity of the original.

This is an academic reinterpretation. It is not affiliated with or approved by the artist or label.

02 / My contribution

One image became a whole package.

What did I make?

I designed the full packaging system myself, from concept to printed mockups.

  • Concept and visual research into Ukiyo-e
  • Two front cover directions, one led by a portrait, one by a landscape
  • Back covers with the 11-track song list
  • Side A and Side B record labels
  • Printed sleeves and vinyl mockups

I had already researched Ukiyo-e's influence on modern graphic design in Floating Worlds in Print. This project put that research into practice.

03 / Design decisions

Calm, poetic, still youthful.

01

Nature as the setting

A great wave, cherry blossoms, cloud forms and a rising sun frame every surface. They bring Ukiyo-e's sense of place and give the album a calm, nostalgic mood.

02

A warm, limited palette

Red-pink and cream, anchored by the deep blue of the waves, echo the restricted inks of woodblock printing while keeping the soft feel of the original album.

03

Type as part of the picture

The brush-script title is drawn into the scene. Vertical panels carry Japanese text, 最初のアルバム (“the first album”) and 光 (“light”), and seal marks sit in the corners like a printmaker's stamp.

The aim was to borrow the discipline of a historical style while keeping the playful personality of Chói.

04 / Process

From one cover to a set that belongs together.

How did the system come together?

I developed two complete sleeve directions, then carried the same palette, borders, type and motifs onto the back covers and both record labels before printing everything as physical mockups.

My files document the finished stages rather than a revision history, so I can show how the system grew, but I can't point to specific changes made after critique.

01 / Portrait-led sleeve

A figure at the center.

The front cover places a pink-haired figure, with an apple balanced on her head, inside a circle of waves. The back cover turns the same scene into a framed track list.

Select the image to see it larger.

Consistency was the real test.

A single strong image is easier than a set. Each sleeve, label and song list had to share color, layout, type, illustration and small details so the package reads as one object.

05 / Outcome and reflection

A full system, not just one image.

What is the real result?

I completed two sleeve directions, back covers with the song list, Side A and Side B labels, and printed vinyl mockups. The project taught me to design a system instead of a single picture, and showed me how a historical style can be reinterpreted in a fresh way while still respecting its origins.

It pushed me to think harder about concept, execution and consistency, and strengthened my skills in album packaging, print layout, visual research, typography and art direction.

Next steps

  3. Language and production reviewHave the Japanese text and Ukiyo-e references checked by knowledgeable readers, then prepare print specifications for the sleeve and labels.
